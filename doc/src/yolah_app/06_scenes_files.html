24.1The game screen's layout

A good example of layout by arithmetic: the panel takes 450 design pixels on the right; the board is the largest multiple of 8 pixels that fits the rest, with its frame and, when shown, the evaluation bar; everything is centred in what remains.

const float margin = 34 * s, panel_w = 450 * s, bar_w = 26 * s, frame = 26 * s, gap = 22 * s;
const float left_w = a.w - panel_w - 3 * margin;
float B = std::min(a.h - 2 * margin - 2 * frame - 10 * s, left_w - bar_w - gap - 2 * frame);
B = std::floor(B / 8) * 8;                         // whole pixels per square: crisp lines
float block_w = (show_eval ? bar_w + gap : 0) + 2 * frame + B;
float bx = margin + (left_w - block_w) / 2 + (show_eval ? bar_w + gap : 0) + frame;
float by = (a.h - B) / 2;
g.board.area = {std::floor(bx), std::floor(by), B, B};
Box panel{a.w - margin - panel_w, margin, panel_w, a.h - 2 * margin};

24.3Puzzles and levels


start_puzzle launches an ordinary game from the puzzle's position (App::launch) with game.puzzle_mode set: the background image replaces the theme's gradient, the puzzle's theme, holes, sprites, tiles and decorations replace the player's, the analysis is off (it would give the solution, except when the editor tests a puzzle), and the game starts with the introduction card. The puzzle's starting bonuses are written into the position's scores (puzzle_position), so the score boxes start from them. At the end, the player's lead is compared with the goal; a success is saved in the settings and opens the next puzzle of the level. During a puzzle, “Recommencer” starts it again from its position (same players, no introduction), and “Indice” proves with the exact solver which of the player's moves still reach the goal (PuzzleHint, prove_moves, the same proofs as the key moves of “Vérifier”, in a thread, 20 s at most): one of them is drawn on the board, the list is told in a message; “too early” with more than 44 free squares, and “no move reaches the goal any more” after a mistake (target goals and early end: 24.5; a game stopped because the player's pieces can no longer move says so, rather than “out of reach against the best defence”). The Puzzles screen only plays: the editor is reached from the main menu. Its puzzle cards show a small picture of the board, always on the light wooden theme (“bois”), where the holes and pieces read best at that size.

24.5Early end and the exact solver as opponent


Early end. A puzzle can stop before the end of the game once its outcome is settled (Puzzle::end_win, end_loss, keys "end on win" and "end on loss": −1 to the end, 0 at once, 1 to 3 player moves later). After each move, a thread (PuzzleOutcome, 4 s budget) settles the new position: won in advance when no moves of either side can prevent the goal (can_fail: a search over every game, cut by a bound: a side can still move to at most the free squares of the free regions next to its pieces, since squares never become free again); lost in advance when the goal is out of reach against the best defence (the exact solver, as “Vérifier”). “Won” is not “feasible”: a feasible puzzle can be won from its first move and must still be played. The game stops (Match::stopped) when the same outcome has lasted for the chosen number of player moves; an open position breaks the streak, a position without an answer in time leaves it as it is. The final card then says why instead of giving the final lead.


Target goals. With the brush “Cible” (key 7), squares of the board become targets (on free squares only; covered by a hole or a piece, a target goes). A target is taken by the piece that lands on it, once: its square is then occupied, then a hole. Two goals use them (Puzzle::goal, key "goal": points, collect, first; "targets": the squares): “Plus de cibles” / “N cibles de plus” (collect: take at least N more targets than the opponent; equal is a draw, so not reached), and “Première cible” (first: the first move onto a target ends the game, won for its player; nobody: a draw). The other AIs play for points, so the opponent of these puzzles is always the exact solver (puzzle_opponent; the editor shows “Solveur exact (imposé)”, and “IA contre IA” uses it on the player's side too); the starting bonuses do not apply. The board shows the targets in red, then a small badge of the taker's colour; the player cards count the targets (the points under them). The solver needs one change for them (EndgameGoal, EndgameSolverOptions::targets): what a move earns is 1 on a target (Collect), a win on a target (First), instead of 1 point; the values still depend on the board only, so the table works as is, and the bounds “one side blocked” count the targets in the regions a side can reach. Checked against a brute-force search: 2320 positions, no difference. “Vérifier” and the early end use the same goals.


The exact solver as opponent. “Solveur exact” (player/solver_player.h, config/solver_player.cfg; roster key "puzzles only": not offered in the new-game screen) searches the game to its end when at most 36 squares are free and plays a move of best final lead, which is the best defence against every goal “win by N”. Before, or when the proof does not finish within 3 × the chosen time, its fallback plays (the dev minimax, embedded by the roster loader from "fallback"). Its table is kept from move to move: after the first proof, the next moves are instantaneous.



24.6Starting bonus and puzzle verification


The bonus. A puzzle can give each side points before the first move (Puzzle::bonus). They are not a separate counter: puzzle_position writes them into the scores of the Yolah position (set_state), exactly as if the side had already made that many moves. So every part of the program that reads the score sees them, the AIs included, without any change to the AIs. What an AI does with them depends on where it reads the score:




WhereSees the bonus?Consequence


end of the game (all AIs)yesa finished game is won by the higher score, bonus included


the dev's exact endgame search (regions, pockets, split scores)yesexact play once the position is small or split into regions


the MCTS's terminal nodesyessame at the leaves that end the game


the NNUE and ResNet evaluationsnothey read the board only (in normal play the score difference is always 0 or 1, they never needed it): with a large bonus on an open board, the AI misjudges the middle game




So a bonus is safe in endgame puzzles (few free squares, or separate regions), and should stay small on an open board. Two uses: a handicap (give points to the weaker side), and goals that the AI really defends. With the goal “Gagner de 3+”, an AI that is already lost has no reason to limit the margin (a loss is a loss for it); with the goal “Gagner” and +2 for the AI, the same puzzle becomes a game the AI fights for, point by point. The same trick makes “survive N moves” puzzles: give the lone piece enough points that surviving long enough is winning, and the AI must shut it in to win.



The verdict. “Vérifier” runs the exact endgame solver (player/endgame_solver.{h,cpp}): a search to the very end of the game, with no evaluation, so its answer is a proof. It answers:


    

  • Faisable: the player reaches the goal against any defence. When the player moves first, the key moves are counted and listed (up to four): the moves after which the goal is still reached. A good puzzle often has a single one; “7 coups clés sur 9” means the puzzle is easy.
    • 

  • Impossible: the best defence stops every attempt (the line says whether it gets a draw or wins).
    • 

  • Inconnu: more than 44 free squares (no attempt), no proof within 10 minutes, or the check stopped (“Vérification arrêtée”): while it runs, the button “Vérifier” becomes “Arrêter”, and the status line shows the time spent. The budget is generous on purpose: how long the proof takes tells the designer how hard the puzzle is. The puzzle then depends on the strength of the AI chosen to play it.
    •

Three choices that make it exact and fast.


    

  1. “Win by N” is checked as “win” with N − 1 more points for the opponent. The lead is ≥ N exactly when the lead minus (N − 1) is ≥ 1. The solver then only has to tell win / draw / loss (a narrow window around 0), which is far cheaper than the exact final score: on the lone-piece puzzle with the goal “Gagner de 3+”, the exact score had no answer in 10 s, the shifted win/loss check answers in 0.07 s.
    2. 

  2. No “pass rule”. The solver has an option saying that a player who must pass while the game is not over has lost. It holds in normal play (if White must pass, Black already has one move more and White will make no other; if Black must pass, the scores are equal and White can still move), and it saves the AlphaZero self-play and the hybrid player a lot of search. But it assumes the scores of a normal game: with a bonus, a blocked player can win, and a puzzle where White moves first at 0–0 does not have those scores either. The editor turns it off. (The dev player has its own copy of the rule, off by default and off in the app's configuration.)
    3. 

  3. Bounds for a lone player. Without the pass rule, a blocked side made the solver slow: the other side then plays alone to the end, and the solver tried every order of its moves to find how many it can make. Two facts give exact bounds instead, whatever the scores: a blocked side stays blocked (a free square never comes back), and every move fills one free square. So when the side to move must pass, the opponent will make at least one more move and at most one per free square it can reach (an 8-connected flood fill from its pieces through free squares); when the opponent is blocked, we will make at least one move and at most one per free square we can reach. When a bound is enough to leave the search window, it is returned at once. On the lone piece with a bonus of 6: 4.6 s → 0.27 s. These bounds are only used when the pass rule is off, so the other users of the solver are unchanged.
    4. 




The parallel solver has its own document, doc/endgame_ybwc.html, and a commented copy, doc/final_ybwc.{h,cpp}.


Several cores. The proof of the verdict shares its work between threads (EndgameSolver::split, “young brothers wait”, as in parallel chess and Othello solvers): at a node with at least 12 free squares, the first move is searched alone, then, if it has not cut and a thread is idle, the other moves are handed out one by one to the idle threads, each on its own copy of the position; a value ≥ beta stops all the threads below that node. The table is shared without a lock: an entry stores key XOR data next to the data, so an entry torn by two simultaneous writes is recognised and ignored, and the proofs stay exact. The key moves are then proved in parallel, one move per thread, each with its own solver. Measured on puzzles of 26 to 28 free squares: 4 to 5.6 times faster than on one core (for example 15.9 s → 2.9 s). The main proof uses at most 8 threads: beyond, the work searched in vain (the moves already started when another one wins) grows faster than the cores it uses. Two details mattered: the counters each thread writes at every node, and the cutoff flag read at every node, each have a cache line of their own (on a shared line, the speed-up fell from 3.3 to 1.9); and a copied Yolah::MoveList keeps its end pointer into the original, so a split point copies its moves into a plain array.


The thread. The check must not freeze the interface, so it runs in a thread of its own (PuzzleCheck, made by puzzle_check_start). A second thread, the watchdog, waits on a condition variable for the result or 10 minutes (PuzzleCheck::BUDGET), then sets the solver's stop flag; the solver sees it at its next node and returns an incomplete result, which becomes “Inconnu”. “Arrêter” sets the same flag. stop and done are always set under the mutex (PuzzleCheck::signal): set without it, a notification could fall between the waiter's test and its sleep and be lost, and the waiter would sleep until its timeout. The job is held by a shared_ptr in the editor's state; its destructor stops and joins both threads, so starting a new check or quitting never leaves a thread behind. The verdict is shown only while the puzzle's key (puzzle_check_key: board, side to move, player's side, goal, bonuses) is the one that was checked.
