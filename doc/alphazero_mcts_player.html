    Yolah MCTS Player Internals

    How the Yolah Monte-Carlo tree search player chooses a move: the value/policy ResNet that guides it, one simulation from the root to a leaf and back, KataGo's exploration formula, the search graph that shares one node per position, the batching that keeps a GPU busy, and the refinements at the root. Every part is shown with its code.

      1The player at a glance

      The minimax dev player looks at millions of positions per move and judges each with a small, fast network. The MCTS player does the opposite: it looks at a few thousand positions, and asks a large network two questions about each one. Who is winning here? (the value head) and which moves deserve a look? (the policy head). The search grows a graph of positions from the root, one simulation at a time, spending its visits on the moves the policy recommends and the values confirm.

      The algorithm is AlphaZero's, with the refinements of KataGo: a log-scaled exploration coefficient scaled again by how much the values disagree, a first-play-urgency rule for the moves not yet tried, a lower confidence bound for the move played, forced playouts and policy target pruning for self-play. On top of that comes one Yolah-specific change: since a position can be reached by many move orders and never repeats, the search stores one node per distinct position and shares it between all the paths that reach it.

      All code excerpts are the real code of the repository. When an excerpt was condensed, its title says so.

      1.1Monte-Carlo tree search with a network

      A simulation starts at the root and walks down the graph. At each position already in the graph it chooses one move by the PUCT rule (section 3), a balance between the moves that look good so far and the moves the policy likes but that have few visits. When it reaches a position that is not in the graph yet, the leaf, it asks the network for the leaf's value and the priors of its moves, adds the leaf to the graph, and sends the value back up along the path: every position on the way gets one more visit and the value, with the sign of the player to move there.

      After thousands of simulations, the visits concentrate on the best moves. The move played is the one the search trusts most (section 6.4). The distribution of the root's visits is the search's improved policy: it is what self-play training uses as the policy target.

      2The network

      The network is a residual convolutional network with two heads, in the style of AlphaZero. It is large next to the NNUE of the minimax player (35 million parameters against 1.3 million) and evaluated far less often: about 4.5 billion operations per position, done in batches on a GPU.

      2.1Inputs: four planes

      The network sees four 8×8 binary planes: the black stones, the white stones, the holes, and a constant plane that is 1 everywhere when white is to move. The score is not an input: the number of holes gives the sum of the two scores, not their split, and the network learns what it needs from the board alone.

      The planes are written in the order of the training cache, element i of a plane being bit 63 − i of the bitboard. Rank 8 lands on the top row, as usual, but the files come out mirrored (H on the left). Nothing in the network minds, since convolutions learn whatever labelling they are shown; what matters is that the C++ encoder and the Python training code agree bit for bit.

      2.2Architecture

      A stem convolution lifts the 4 planes to 256 channels; 30 residual blocks follow, each two 3×3 convolutions with BatchNorm and ReLU before each convolution (pre-activation, ResNet v2); a final BatchNorm and ReLU closes the trunk. Both heads read the same 256 × 8 × 8 feature map.

      The value is the expected result for the side to move, in (−1, 1): +1 a win, 0 a draw, −1 a loss. The policy is a vector of 4096 logits, one per pair of squares, indexed by 64 · from + to with the true square numbers. About three quarters of these pairs are never legal; training never targets them and the search ignores them.

      2.3From logits to priors

      The evaluator returns the logits of exactly the legal moves, in the order of Yolah::moves. The search turns them into priors with a softmax over those moves only, with an optional temperature (1 in every configuration).

      2.4Two backends behind one interface

      The search never touches the network directly. It calls nn::Evaluator::evaluate with a span of requests (a position and its legal moves) and receives a span of results (a value and the logits of the legal moves). Two backends implement it:

      • nn::ResNetTorch loads a TorchScript export (.ts) and runs it with libtorch on the GPU, in fp16. On an RTX 3080 it evaluates 8 000 to 10 000 positions per second at batch 256. It is the backend of every match and of self-play.
      • nn::ResNetCPU loads a binary export in which each BatchNorm is folded into the convolution before it, and runs the convolutions with Eigen and OpenMP: 130 to 160 positions per second at batch 32. It exists for machines without a GPU and for testing.

      The JSON key backend picks one; with more than one search thread, the player wraps it in a BatchingEvaluator (section 5.3).

      3One simulation

      A simulation is a descent, an evaluation, an expansion and a backup. This section follows one simulation in a single thread; section 5 shows how many of them run at once.

      3.1What a node and an edge hold

      The graph has two kinds of objects. A node is a position: its visit count N, the sum W of the values backed up through it and the sum W2 of their squares, the network's value at the position, and its edges. An edge is a move from a node: its prior P, a pointer to the successor node (created on the first descent through the edge), and the edge's own visit count. The difference between the node's N and the edge's N matters as soon as a position has several parents (section 4).

      W is stored for the player who moved into the node. Then W(child) / N(child) is directly the Q that PUCT needs at the parent: the value of the move for the player choosing it.

      3.2Choosing a move: PUCT in KataGo's form

      At an expanded node the descent takes the edge with the highest score:

      score(a) = Q(a) + c(s) · P(a) · √(W(s) + 0.01) / (1 + N(a)) c(s) = [ c + c_log · log((W(s) + c_base) / c_base) ] · fσ(s) c = 1, c_log = 0.45, c_base = 500 W(s) = Σ_a N(a): the visits of the node's edges (with the pending ones, 5.2)

      The first term is exploitation: the average value of the successor. The second is exploration: large for a move with a high prior and few visits, shrinking like 1 / N(a) as the move is visited, growing like √W(s) as its siblings are. A move with a 30 % prior is visited again and again unless its value turns out bad; a move with a 1 % prior needs its siblings to collect many visits before it gets one.

      AlphaZero uses a constant c. KataGo adds the log term, which raises c slowly as the node gets more visits (1.0 at 0 visits, 1.49 at 1000, 2.37 at 10 000), so a well explored node keeps looking at its alternatives, and the factor fσ of section 3.3.

      3.3Exploring where the values disagree: fσ

      The second KataGo factor scales the exploration by the standard deviation of the values backed up through the node, against a prior of 0.40:

      fσ(s) = 1 + 0.85 · (stdev(s) / 0.40 − 1)

      Where the backed-up values agree (a position whose outcome is settled, stdev near 0), fσ falls to 0.15 and the search exploits; where they disagree (a sharp position, stdev 0.6), fσ rises to 1.43 and the search looks wider. The stdev is regularised towards the prior with a weight of two visits, so a node with few visits keeps fσ near 1. Node W2, the sum of the squared values, is what makes this possible; the sign of the values does not matter in a square, so W2 is the same at every ply.

      3.4Moves not tried yet: first play urgency

      A move without visits has no Q. Taking 0 would make every untried move of a lost position look attractive, and the search would spread over all of them. KataGo's first play urgency gives it the value of the parent, lowered in proportion to how much of the policy has already been tried:

      FPU = baseline − reduction · √m reduction = 0.2 (root: 0.1) baseline = m · Q(parent) + (1 − m) · v_net(parent) m = prior mass of the moves already visited

      At a fresh node (nothing tried, m = 0) an untried move is worth the network's value of the parent. Once the moves covering 80 % of the policy have been tried, the next one is worth the parent's average lowered by 0.2 · √0.8 ≈ 0.18: a move the policy did not like has to be good to be preferred to the known ones. At the root the reduction is halved, so the search looks a little wider there.

      3.5Expansion and backup

      At the leaf, the network's value v is for the player to move there. The leaf's W is for the player who moved into it, so the leaf receives −v, its parent +v, and so on, the sign flipping at every ply: the negamax convention. Each node on the path gets one more visit, and so does each edge taken. A finished game is scored exactly (+1, 0, −1 by the final score) and backed up the same way, without a network call.

      A pass is an ordinary move here: the side that cannot move plays the pass move (index 0, A1 → A1, never a real move), the turn changes, and the signs keep alternating with the plies.

      4A graph, not a tree

      In Yolah, every move turns the square the stone leaves into a hole. The number of holes grows by exactly one per move, so a position can never come back: the positions form a directed acyclic graph. At the same time, the four stones of a side move independently, so move orders commute and the same position is reached along many paths. A tree search would build one copy of a position per path; the search stores one node per distinct position instead.

      4.1One node per position

      The nodes live in a hash table of 64 shards, each a mutex and an unordered_map from the position key to the node. When a descent takes an edge whose successor does not exist yet, it looks the position up under the shard lock: either another path already created it, and the edge is linked to the existing node (a transposition), or a new node is created. Nodes are created lazily, one per descent: at 400 simulations the search allocates about 400 nodes, where an eager expansion of every child would allocate about 20 000.

      4.2Q from the node, N from the edge

      A shared node accumulates the visits of all its parents. PUCT then reads two different counts. Q is the node's average: everything the search knows about the position, whichever parent paid for it. The visit count in the exploration term is the edge's: how much of this parent's exploration has gone into the move. A position refuted under one parent is refuted under every parent at once, and a parent does not stop exploring a move just because another parent visited the same position often. This is KataGo's graph search with every visit weighted 1.

      4.3The node key

      The Zobrist hash covers the board and the side to move: exactly what the network sees, which is why the network cache keys on it alone. It does not cover how the points scored so far split between the players. Two positions with the same board can differ there (a pass scores nothing), and the final result depends on the split, so the node key mixes black's score into the hash.

      4.4Keeping the graph from one move to the next

      At the next move, the position to search is usually already in the graph: two plies deeper, after the opponent's reply. The node table makes reuse a single lookup: if the key is there, that node becomes the root with all its statistics, however deep it was. Then a mark-and-sweep pass frees every node the new root cannot reach.

      4.5What sharing buys

      Measured with the CPU backend, one thread, 3200 simulations on four positions: in tree mode 7 to 20 % of the leaves are transpositions. The tree search evaluates them again for free from the network cache, which refreshes a path but brings no new information. The graph search links to the existing node and goes on to a position nobody has evaluated. Per simulation it is 5 to 15 % slower (each simulation now costs a real network call), but it examines 20 to 25 % more distinct positions for the same simulation count, about 4 to 8 % more per second, and every shared node pools the statistics of all its parents.

      5Many simulations at once

      A GPU computes a batch of 256 positions in barely more time than one. A single simulation at a time would leave it idle almost always. The search therefore runs several threads, each gathering a batch of leaves before one network call, and a batching evaluator merges the threads' batches into a larger one.

      5.1Gather, evaluate, expand

      Each thread repeats three steps until the clock or the simulation budget runs out:

      1. Gather up to batch size leaves: descend, as in section 3, but do not wait for the network at the leaf. Mark it EXPANDING and start the next descent. A finished game is backed up at once; a leaf found in the network cache is expanded at once (5.4).
      2. Evaluate the batch with one call to the evaluator.
      3. Expand each leaf with its priors, store the output in the cache, and back up every descent along its own path.

      Two descents of the same batch can reach the same leaf, under graph search even through different parents. Each descent keeps its own path, and the leaf's value is backed up once per descent.

      5.2Virtual loss and collisions

      Without a correction, all the descents of a batch would follow the same path to the same leaf: nothing changes in the statistics until the network answers. Each descent therefore adds a pending visit to every node and edge on its path, and PUCT counts each pending visit as a visit that returned a loss (−1 in the node's Q, +1 in the edge's N). The next descent sees the path as worse and more visited, and goes elsewhere. The backup turns each pending visit into a real one with the network's value.

      A descent that reaches a leaf another thread is already evaluating is a collision. It is undone (its pending visits removed), and after batch size collisions the batch is closed early. A descent that reaches a leaf of its own batch rides along on the same evaluation.

      The price of descents in flight. With 16 threads × batch 64, up to 1024 descents are pending at once, and the virtual losses distort the statistics more and more. In a tournament at 1 s per move on crn25 (L40S), the MCTS players with 16 × 16 (256 in flight) scored about 56 Elo more against the NNUE player g046 than the same players with 16 × 64; the GPU throughput was not the limit. 16 × 16 is the competitive setting since.

      5.3The batching evaluator

      Each thread hands its batch to the BatchingEvaluator and blocks. One worker thread launches a merged batch as soon as the queued positions reach the merged size (threads × batch), or every thread is waiting, or 500 µs have passed with something queued. It runs the backend once and scatters the results back. A thread whose descents all collided joins with an empty request, a barrier: it wakes up after the next batch, when the leaves it collided with are expanded.

      5.4The network cache

      The network's output depends only on the board and the side to move, so it is cached under the Zobrist hash: the value and the priors of the legal moves, stored as 16-bit fixed point. The table is direct-mapped (always overwrite), lock-striped, and checks the number of legal moves as well as the key. It catches the transpositions the node table missed (positions freed by the garbage collection) and survives from one move to the next and from one game to the next. It is cleared when the weights change.

      6At the root

      Four things happen at the root and nowhere else. Two of them only in self-play: the noise and the forced playouts, which make the search try moves the network underrates. Two always: the pruning of the play values, and the choice of the move by a lower confidence bound.

      6.1Dirichlet noise

      In self-play the root priors are mixed with Dirichlet noise: P' = 0.75 · P + 0.25 · Dir(0.3). The noise is drawn once per search, only on full searches (6.5), and is off in matches.

      6.2Forced playouts

      Noise makes a move interesting; one bad evaluation would then be enough for PUCT to drop it. KataGo forces every root move that has been visited at least once up to √(k · P · W) visits, with k = 2 in self-play: with W = 1600 root visits, a move with a prior of 5 % gets at least √(2 · 0.05 · 1600) ≈ 13 visits.

      6.3Policy target pruning

      The forced visits must not end up in the training target, or the network would learn to like the moves the noise picked. After the search, the play values start as the root's edge visits. For every move other than the best one, the code inverts the PUCT formula: how many visits would PUCT have given this move, at most, for its score to stay below the best move's? The visits beyond that count are removed. A move whose value alone beats the best move's score keeps all its visits. These pruned values, normalised, are the policy target π.

      E = c(root) · fσ(root) · √(W + 0.01) best_score = Q(best) + E · P(best) / (1 + N(best)) wanted(a) = E · P(a) / (best_score − Q(a)) − 1 play(a) = min(N(a), ⌈wanted(a)⌉)

      6.4The move played: a lower confidence bound

      The most visited move is usually the best, but a move with fewer visits and a clearly better Q may be better still. KataGo plays the move with the best lower confidence bound Q − 5 · σ(Q), among the moves with at least 20 % of the best move's visits. σ(Q) comes from the child's W2: the spread of the values backed up through it, divided by the square root of its visits. The winner's play value is raised just enough to beat the others, so the move with the highest play value is the move played. π is copied before this step: the LCB says which move to play, not how the visits were spread.

      6.5Self-play: playout cap randomization and temperature

      Self-play needs many games for the value head and good searches for the policy target. KataGo's playout cap randomization gives both: each move is a full search (1600 simulations, noise, forced playouts, π recorded) with probability 0.25, and a fast search (200 simulations, no noise, no π) otherwise. Only the full searches produce policy targets; all the positions get the game's result. In the first 20 plies the move is drawn with probability proportional to π (temperature 1), so that the games differ; after that the best move is played.

      7Strength

      The network of the competitive player came from two sources. The supervised ResNet was trained on the engine games of data/games (the result for the value, the move played for the policy); the AlphaZero 256 run then trained it further by self-play for about four weeks on the cluster.
